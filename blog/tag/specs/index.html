Meet the E-2C Hawkeye: the Screwtops, by the numbers 2e1b3aa

She’s the newest silhouette over the bay and the highest flier in the pattern: the Grumman E-2C Hawkeye of Airborne Command and Control Squadron 123 — VAW-123, the SCREWTOPS — now wears her squadron mark, the black corkscrew around the rotodome. The dome itself got its geometry sorted this push: a horizontal pancake on its twin struts, spinning about its vertical axis exactly as the real radar does. Her card:

  1. Crew: five. Two pilots up front, three mission specialists in the back — radar officer, control officer, radar operator — the team that talks the fighters onto the bandits.
  2. Powerplant: two Allison T56-A-427 turboprops, 5,100 shp apiece, driving four-bladed props. Max speed around 350 kt, but her living is the loiter: five to six hours on station.
  3. Dimensions: 80 ft 7 in span, 57 ft 9 in long — and the rotodome spans 24 feet, bigger than some fighters’ wings.
  4. Ceiling: 34,700 ft. Range: about 1,460 nautical miles. High enough and patient enough to see the whole theatre.
  5. The kit: AN/APS-145 radar in the dome — tracks thousands of contacts out past 300 miles, friend or foe, sea level or stratosphere.
  6. The squadron: VAW-123 “Screwtops,” established 1967 out of Norfolk — hence the black barber-pole spiral painted around the dome rim, three turns of pure squadron pride. It spins with the radar.
  7. In the bay: she orbits at angels 23 and works the radio the way a real Hawkeye does — BOGEY with clock code, miles and angels; CIVIL TRAFFIC conflicts; FRIENDLIES round-ups; a PICTURE every couple of minutes. Bullseye is Alcatraz. Find her on gallery page 14/14, or spectate her wheel with J.

Meet the SH-60 Seahawk: the cruiserhref="/blog/sh60-seahawk">Meet the SH-60 Seahawk: the cruiser’s rotor, by the numbers PENDINGrsquo;s rotor, by the numbers 2e1b3aa

The thump you hear crossing the cruiser’s wake is her: the Sikorsky SH-60 Seahawk, the fleet’s shipboard helicopter, and the first rotor in Hornet Bay. She spools up on the guided-missile cruiser’s aft deck, flies a four-leg circuit of the group, and puts it back down on a deck that’s still making 15 knots — while two of her sisters shuttle the bay’s five new helipads. Her card:

  1. Crew: three to four — pilot, co-pilot, and sensor/rescue crew in the back.
  2. Powerplant: two GE T700-GE-401 turboshafts, about 1,700 shp each, turning a 53 ft 8 in main rotor.
  3. Performance: 180 kt flat out, 146 kt cruise, with a service ceiling near 12,000 ft and about 450 nautical miles of legs.
  4. Dimensions: 64 ft 10 in nose to tail — compact enough for a frigate’s deck, big enough to haul a torpedo and a winchman.
  5. The kit, by variant: sonobuoys and a MAD bird on the SH-60B, dipping sonar on the SH-60F, Mk 46/50 torpedoes for the kill — plus plane guard, SAR and VERTREP, because somebody has to pick up what the fixed-wings drop.
  6. In the bay: full rotor spool-up with the blur disc fading in past 62% rpm, a moving-deck approach that chases the ship’s own motion, and pad bookings on the shuttle routes — SFO, Oakland, Moffett, Alameda and the SF Heliport. Gallery page 13/14; spectate the circuit with J.

Meet the P-3 Orion: the sub hunter, by the numbers 2e1b3aa

Out to the west of the carrier group, low and slow and endlessly patient, flies the Lockheed P-3 Orion — the Navy’s maritime patrol workhorse since 1962, derived from the Electra airliner and hunting submarines for over sixty years. Ours works an 11×5 km oval at 500 feet and breaks off now and then to eyeball a surface contact up close. Her card:

  1. Crew: eleven — two pilots, a flight engineer, and a full sensor and ordnance team down the back.
  2. Powerplant: four Allison T56-A-14 turboprops, 4,600 shp each — the same engine family as our Hawkeye, twice as many of them.
  3. Performance: 411 kt max, but she earns her pay at 180–200 kt, 500 to 1,500 feet, ten hours on station.
  4. Dimensions: 99 ft 8 in span, 116 ft 10 in long — the biggest aircraft in our gallery that isn’t an airliner.
  5. Ceiling: 28,300 ft. Range: about 2,380 nautical miles.
  6. The kit: the magnetic anomaly detector in the tail stinger — the MAD boom you can see on our model — sonobuoys by the dozen, Mk 46/50 torpedoes and Harpoons in the bay and on the wings.
  7. In the bay: radar-visible but nobody’s target, she announces every visual inspection pass on the radio — 400 metres abeam the contact at 300 feet, then back to the oval. Gallery page 12/14; spectate the hunt with J.
