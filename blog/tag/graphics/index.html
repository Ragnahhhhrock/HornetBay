  2. Torpedoes under silk. The P-3 and the S-3 run the datum together now — and this time they don’t just listen. Each drops a Mark 46 with a retarder chute that cracks open the instant the weapon leaves the rack, brakes it to a soft splashdown, then collapses on the sea while the fish runs hot toward the contact. Two wakes converge on the prowler and the water comes up.
  3. The downtown burner run. Four Hornets in a tight diamond, burners lit, 300 knots across the rooftops at night — fast cuts between a swinging arc off the formation, a rooftop that gets buzzed by twenty feet, the Transamerica perch as they carve past below the tip, and the whole lit skyline receding behind four orange glows.

The reel: five scenes of trouble behind the menu, and a key to the whole board b739eb6

The old attract mode was one Hornet touring the bay at a polite 210 knots. Polite is over. The menu now backs onto a five-scene cinematic reel — a director that cuts between carrier ops, a dogfight and a bridge run, day, dusk and night, with a flash cut between takes:

  1. The cat shot, at night, full burner. Steam wisping off the track, deck crew in their jerseys, and the Hornet goes from the hold to 78 metres a second in 128 feet of deck — twin fireballs you can read from the LSO platform. The camera rides the bow ramp for the tension, alongside for the shot, then chases the climb-out.
  2. The furball. A MiG-29 and a Hornet circle over the bay. The bandit shoots first — the Hornet flares it off, pulls the reversal, and its Sidewinder does not miss. Watch the fireball, the smoke bloom and the death spiral into the headlands; the camera rides with the winner until the kill, then with the loser all the way down.
  3. The trap, at dusk, under a full moon. A Tomcat in the groove, hook down, 2.4 kilometres of final at three and a half degrees — into the wires, smoke off the deck, rollout on the angle.
  4. The fleet flyby. An F-14, an F-16 and an A-10 in echelon come down the deck axis at 250 knots and forty metres — burner glows on, sonic crack as they cross the island.
  5. The bridge run. Night again, 290 knots at 46 metres, straight through the Golden Gate gap with the burners lit — then the pull, vertical over the towers, vapor puffing off the wingtips. The camera rides the left wing the whole way.
  6. Every scene owns its sky. The reel sets its own time of day per take (the menu day/night toggle no longer fights it), and the burners got the cinematic treatment — bigger, angrier flames than gameplay, with a glow sprite that reads from any angle, day or night.
  7. And a key to the whole board: MALCOLMSONLY. Press U on the menu and enter the code — every school sortie and every campaign mission signs open from the first boot, no syllabus, no progression. Named for the skipper’s toughest critic. The board reads ALL ACCESS when it takes.

The Enterprise gets her angled deck 150c3fa

FEATUREGRAPHICS

Sharp eyes over the round-down spotted it: the Big E’s deck still wore a straight axial centreline down the middle, which read as the runway. No more — like every real carrier, her runway is now unmistakably the angled deck:

  1. The straight centreline is gone. What remains is the one true runway: the angled landing strip, offset to port so pilots land left of the island — the whole point of the angled deck since 1952.
  2. The strip got the full treatment — bolder edge stripes, bigger centreline dashes, and a threshold bar across the stern end where the pass begins.
  3. Everything still agrees: the arrestor wires, the IFLOLS meatball and the landing aim-point all derive from the same strip geometry, so what you see is exactly what you trap on. Verified from the overhead: strip angles off the ship’s line and passes port of the island, wires square across it.
  4. The printable carrier wears it too — the six-angle shots in the Print Bay were re-taken on the new deck plan.
261200Z JUL 26

Weather: clouds, storms and lightning 8c11d77

FEATUREGRAPHICSAUDIO

The R toggle just grew a full weather ladder. Where it once flipped CLEAR/RAIN it now steps through five states — MISSION DEFAULT → CLEAR → CLOUDS → RAIN → STORM — on the main menu, on the plane-select map, and saved between visits:

  1. CLOUDS breaks the original’s cloudless sky — the scattered sprite deck that ships with the sim finally gets to fly, fading in over a couple of seconds with a light dimming of the day. Everything eases; nothing pops.
  2. RAIN implies overcast now — the streak rain you know, under the cloud deck, with the usual gloom dragging the murk in close.
  3. STORM brings the lightning. Every few seconds a jagged channel bolt walks down out of the deck somewhere within a few miles, the whole sky answers with a cold blue-white flash, and the thunder arrives late — delayed by true distance at 343 m/s, a crack decaying into a long low rumble through the audio graph.
  4. Clouds got their own weather channel — separate from the rain blend, so overcast days can exist without a drop of rain, and every state fades smoothly through the same sky/fog/sun gloom the rain always used.
  5. Manners kept: satellite planning maps stay free of rain, murk and bolts; pausing still silences the sky; and missions you authored stay exactly as clear as you left them — MISSION DEFAULT never surprises you.
