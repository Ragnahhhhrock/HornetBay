Meet the A-6E Intruder: Knight Rider, by the numbers 86ef63b

The first of three shipmates reporting aboard this push is the fleet’s all-weather truck: an A-6E Intruder of VA-52, the Knight Riders — tail code NL, modex 501, straight off the 1994 Pacific cruise. Ours answers to KNIGHT 501. Her card:

  1. Crew: two, side by side — pilot left, bombardier/navigator right and a half-step back. The wide framed greenhouse is the Intruder’s face.
  2. Powerplant: two Pratt & Whitney J52 turbojets, about 9,300 lbf each, buried in the fuselage with cheek intakes. No afterburners — the A-6 was all about hauling bombs through weather, not speed.
  3. The kit: DIANE attack radar in that dark nose, and on the E-model the chin TRAM turret under the radome — the little ball our model carries. The fixed refuelling probe spears up off the nose: you can’t mistake the silhouette.
  4. 1994 squadron: VA-52 Knight Riders, NAS Whidbey Island, Carrier Air Wing 15 — the Pacific fleet’s Intruders in their final seasons. VA-52 stood down in April 1995; only the Sunday Punchers flew the type longer.
  5. Dimensions: 54 ft 9 in long, 53-foot span, rated for 18,000 lb of ordnance — the medium-attack muscle of its day.
  6. In the bay: KNIGHT 501 parks on the Big E’s starboard row, taxis to Cat 2, launches off the bow, flies the pattern and traps back aboard — around the clock. Friendly blue on radar and the map (N), spectate-able with J, and a printable STL on the store.

Second aboard is the hardest-working airframe in the battle group — the Carrier Onboard Delivery bird, a C-2A Greyhound of VRC-30, the Providers: tail code RW, modex 30, answering to PROVIDER 30. Mail, parts, passengers — if it matters, the COD brings it. The card:

Meet the A-10 Warthog: the Hawg, by the numbers 86ef63b

And the second Air Force arrival needs no introduction — only a long pause for effect. The A-10 Thunderbolt II, the Warthog, joins as a player mount on 5. It is slow. It is honest. It is built around the largest gun ever bolted into an aeroplane. The card:

  1. The gun: GAU-8 Avenger, seven barrels of 30 mm at up to 4,200 rounds a minute. Ours carries 1,150 — the real combat load — and it is the default weapon, as it should be.

  4. The wing: long, straight, thick, built for slow loiter over the fight — the opposite philosophy to the Eagle, and proud of it. Stall speed low enough to read headstones.
  5. 1994 colours: gunship grey with DM on the fins — the 355th Wing out of Davis-Monthan — and a pair of Sidewinders for self-escort, because even the Hawg carries a knife.
  6. In the bay: runway starts only, no hook, no bridle, no boat — the Air Force way. Try the low pass down the estuary. Gallery page 5, printable STL on the store.

Meet the Su-27 Flanker: the new bogey, by the numbers 86ef63b

The MiG-29 has been asking for backup, and the Soviets have answered: the Su-27 Flanker is in the game — red stars on the fins, blue 38 on the nose, and a very long memory for grudges. It has no missions yet. It is simply out there, waiting. The card:

  1. Powerplant: two Saturn AL-31Fs, about 27,500 lbf each in afterburner, widely spaced with the famous tail stinger between the nozzles.
  2. The shape: long drooped nose, big blended wing with leading-edge extensions, twin canted tails on the engine nacelles — one of the most elegant fighters ever drawn, and one of the largest.
  3. The party trick: Pugachev’s Cobra — a full-poststall pitch past vertical that the big Sukhoi simply shrugs off. If you see one do it in the merge, you are already behind.
  4. 1994 colours: Soviet-era grey-blue with red stars and a blue bort number, the same generation of markings our Fulcrum wears — front-line PVO kit before the roundels changed.
  5. Dimensions: 71 ft 10 in long, 48 ft 3 in span — twelve feet longer than your Hornet, and it turns like it hasn’t heard.
  6. In the bay: a bogie for missions we have yet to fly — it spawns and fights like the MiG. For now, find it on the showroom floor (page 7), watch for the stars, and print one from the store if you want a head start studying it.

The flight deck is no longer a quiet place to be left alone with your thoughts. The Enterprise now works her 1994 Pacific air wing around the clock — three period squadrons flying a continuous launch–pattern–trap–park cycle whether you are watching or not:

  1. KNIGHT 501 — the VA-52 A-6E Intruder — SAWBUCK 700, the VS-37 S-3B Viking, and PROVIDER 30, the VRC-30 C-2 Greyhound, park on the starboard row, taxi the safe lane to Cat 2 (Cat 1 stays yours), and get flung off the bow in turn.
  2. They fly the pattern like professionals: climb off the bow, the break, a port-side downwind at 850 feet, base, and the final gate — then down the 3.5° groove to the angle deck and the 3-wire. Rollout, taxi clear, park, and go again.
  3. The radio knows the script: CAT 2 — YOU’RE AWAY on the shot, BOLTER, BOLTER if the wires are missed (they go around, honestly), and TRAPPED ABOARD when the hook takes. The feed calls the 3-wire by name.
  4. Discipline, even ambient: the air wing holds a lap if you are on final, and only one jet works the groove at a time — the rest stack politely in the pattern. Taxi routes respect your spot on Cat 1.
  5. Everywhere a friendly should be: blue contacts on radar with their callsigns, named on the tactical map (N), and spectate-able with J — ride the COD down the groove from the LSO’s seat sometime.
  6. Why 1994 matters: all three squadrons were the real Pacific operators that summer — VA-52 and VS-37 in CVW-15, VRC-30 Det 1 flying its first cruise — researched, liveried, and parked on the wrong carrier entirely, as tradition allows.   2. So he went Army. Re-mustered as an aerial observer, then front-seat gunner on the AH-64 — the seat where the shooting actually happens. The chain gun under the Apache’s chin slaved to his helmet: where Clarry looks, thirty millimetres of attention follows.

Anytime, baby: the F-14 Tomcat joins the squadron 29c4432

The flight line just grew a third mount, and she’s a big one. The Grumman F-14 Tomcat — fleet defender, swing-wing icon, and the only fighter ever to operationally sling the AIM-54 Phoenix — is ready for catapult shots off the Big E, wearing the 1978 VF-84 Jolly Rogers scheme: gull grey over white, black tails with yellow tips, skull and crossbones on both rudders, AJ on the modex:

  1. She’s fully carrier-qualified. Cat 1 holdback and stroke off the bow, tailhook and wires back aboard — the same boat ops as the Hornet, with the Tomcat’s heavier, thirstier twin-engine personality (and a higher ceiling: those TF30s breathe up to 50,000 ft).
  2. The wings sweep on command. S works the swing-wing: 20° spread for the pattern, 68° swept for the dash, easing over about two seconds just like the real box. The flight model morphs with the geometry — drag drops, stall speed climbs, roll sharpens, the G limit rises — and the HUD calls out the sweep angle as it travels. She even oversweeps to park, wings hugging the fuselage on the deck.
  3. Four AIM-54 Phoenix in the tunnel, two Sidewinders on the glove pylons. The Phoenix is a different class of weapon from anything else in the bay: fatter, faster, and fired from far beyond the horizon — radar-guided all the way, FOX 3. It rides the rails from 1.2 out to 90 kilometres.
  4. The AWG-9 reaches out. The Tomcat’s radar adds a 100 MI tier on top of the usual 40/10/2 — R cycles the lot, and the scope, the lock logic and the Phoenix’s envelope all honour the big radar’s legs.
